Reducing Decision Friction in Streaming Discovery

Why 1 in 5 people give up on picking something to watch, and what I'd try.

What bugged me
People in the US say they spend about 12 minutes looking for something to watch, and about 1 in 5 streaming viewers say they'd give up on watching if the search doesn't work out. If you've ever closed the app and opened Instagram instead, you're in that group.
What I learned
Netflix already adjusts for things like time of day and the device you're on. What it can't easily know is what you need tonight: that you're wiped out, only have half an hour, or have someone on the couch with you. There's no quick way to tell it.
What I'd do
When someone has been clicking around for a while without picking anything, add one row with three picks, each with a short reason, plus an easy way to say what you're after tonight. Then test whether it helps people find something they're glad they picked.

The problem

The 9pm scroll

You probably know the routine. It's a weeknight, you open Netflix, scroll past thirty-something titles, open two of them, back out of both, and ten minutes later you're on your phone.

This isn't a niche problem. In Gracenote's 2025 State of Play survey of 3,000 streaming viewers in six countries, people in the US said they spend about 12 minutes searching for something to watch, up from 10.5 minutes in 2023. About one in five said they'd abandon the session if their search didn't turn anything up, and 45% called the whole streaming experience overwhelming.

It may also affect whether people stay. In the same survey, 49% said they'd be willing to cancel a service because it's hard to find something to watch.

These are things people say in a survey, across all streaming services. They aren't Netflix's numbers, and saying you'd cancel isn't the same as cancelling. Still, the direction is hard to ignore.

the one that stuck with me: nearly half would think about cancelling over this.

Where I was wrong

I assumed it was too many choices

My starting point was the explanation everyone reaches for: Netflix has so much stuff that people freeze. So I went looking for the research behind it, expecting it to be solid.

It wasn't, at least not in the simple version. When researchers pooled 50 experiments in 2010, the average choice-overload effect came out close to zero. Some studies found a strong effect and plenty found none.

Research on recommendations tells a similar story. A 2010 study found that showing people a bigger set of good recommendations didn't make them more satisfied with what they picked. And a 2025 field experiment with 1.6 million online shoppers found that buying went up and then down as more recommended products were shown. Up to 64% of that drop came from people not clicking on anything at all.

Neither study tells you the right number of picks for Netflix, and the second one is about shopping, not TV. What they did change is the question I was asking. I stopped asking how many options is too many and started asking what makes someone feel sure enough to start.

A 2015 review helped with that, because it looked at when overload actually shows up: when the options are hard to compare, when the decision feels like it matters, when people aren't sure what they want, and when they need to pick right now instead of just looking around. Read that list again and it sounds a lot like a weeknight on the couch:

What the research saysWhat it sounds like at 9pm
People aren't sure what they want
"I honestly don't know what I feel like watching."
They need to pick right now
"I've got an hour before bed. Something, please."
The decision feels like it matters
"What if I start this and it's bad?"

this is where the project changed direction. I stopped thinking about showing people more and started thinking about how to make them feel sure about one thing.

What's been tried

Netflix has been working on this for years

Before suggesting anything, I wanted to see what Netflix had already tried.

  1. 2021Fast Laughs. A vertical feed of comedy clips on mobile, pitched as a way to discover shows.
  2. 2021Play Something. One button, and Netflix picks something for you.
  3. January 2023Play Something is removed. Not enough people used it.
  4. 2026Clips. A personalized vertical feed of previews in the redesigned mobile app.

Two things stood out to me. Play Something suggests people don't want to hand the decision over completely, even when they're stuck. And Clips lives on phones. Netflix said back in 2018 that about 70% of its streams happened on TVs. I couldn't find a recent public number, so designing for the TV is my choice of focus rather than something I can prove is the bigger problem.

one went away, the other lives on phones. neither really helps someone stuck on the couch at 9pm.

The idea

Netflix knows my usual Tuesday. It doesn't know this one.

Netflix already pays attention to context. Its help center says recommendations take into account the time of day, the device you're on, how long you watch and whether you finish things, with recent viewing counting more than older viewing. Researchers have argued for this for years: what's worth recommending depends on circumstances like when you're watching and who's with you.

What Netflix can't see is how you feel tonight. Psychologists who study media choice have found that people pick entertainment partly to change their mood. In one classic experiment, stressed people chose more relaxing programs and bored people chose more exciting ones. So "I usually like thrillers" and "I want something easy tonight" can both be true.

My hypothesis: some stuck sessions happen when what you need right now doesn't match what the app has guessed. If that's right, giving people a quick way to say so should help.

Nothing I found proves that stuck nights are unusual nights. It's the first thing I'd want to check.

if this one is wrong, most of the rest falls apart too

Two options

Two ways I could have gone

my pick

A feed for stuck viewers

Send people who seem stuck into a vertical feed of clips.

Going for itIt's engaging, and Netflix has mostly built it already with Clips.

Against itIt's on phones, it adds even more things to choose from, and clips designed to hook you can oversell a show.

my pick ↓

Tonight's three

When someone seems stuck, add one row with three picks, each with a reason, and an easy way to say what you want tonight.

Going for itIt works on TV, it needs no new content, and it goes after the actual problem, which is not feeling sure.

Against itIt depends on spotting stuck people correctly, and it can't end up burying new shows.

I went with the second one, mostly because of the TV point, and because Play Something suggests people still want to be the ones choosing.

The design

One evening, three moments

Here's how it would play out for one person on a Tuesday night. Scroll through it, or tap "Lighter tonight" in the screens to try it yourself. The shows and numbers are made up.

9:02pmbrowsing, not picking

nothing changes on screen yet. behind the scenes, the app notices someone hopping between titles for over two minutes without picking, which is longer than they usually take.

9:04pmthe row shows up

one row slides in right where their focus already is. the rows they were browsing are still there, just underneath.

9:05pmone tap: lighter tonight

their taste stays the same, the picks just get lighter and shorter. the comfort pick doesn't move because it already fit.

Each of the three picks has a job:

  • Safe bet. The strongest match for you. Netflix already uses whether people finish titles as a signal, so "people like you finished this" could be one part of how confident the pick is. Finishing something doesn't prove you loved it, though, so it shouldn't be the only part.
  • New for you. A slot saved for new releases, so they don't get pushed out by older hits.
  • Comfort. Something familiar, like the next episode of a show you're already in the middle of.

The chips ask what you want ("lighter," "short"), not how you feel ("tired"). Mood research is as much about people changing their mood as matching it, so "I'm tired" doesn't tell you whether someone wants a comedy or something calm. Which kind of question people find easier to answer is something I'd test.

I'd also test the one-line reasons. Research on recommendation explanations found that a reason can make a pick more appealing without helping people choose better. So I'd compare no reason, a reason about tonight ("fits your 30 minutes") and a reason about history ("because you watched The Long Dark").

Let the picks adjust on their own first. The chips are a backup, and if nobody uses them, they go.

How I'd test it

What I'd actually do first

Phase 0 · 2–3 weeks · no engineering

Check that the problem is real

Look at how often people browse for a couple of minutes without picking anything, and whether people who do that a lot are more likely to cancel. Then ask 20–30 people about the last time they had trouble choosing:

  • What happened?
  • How were you feeling, who were you with, and how much time did you have? Was that night typical for you?
  • What did you end up doing, and why?

Also ask about a night when choosing was easy, so there's something to compare against.

Only keep going if stuck sessions are common and the stories point to mismatched needs, rather than something else, like nothing good in the catalog.

Phase 1 · 4–5 weeks

Ship just the row

Split people three ways: no row, the row for everyone, and the row only when someone looks stuck. For each, measure three separate things:

  • Did they engage with any of the three picks at all?
  • Did they start something and watch it for 10+ minutes?
  • When asked, did they say it was a good pick for them?

Also watch: plays of new titles, how much of it is rewatching, and whether people who weren't stuck find it annoying.

Phase 2

Add the extras one at a time

The completion signal, the chips, the reason wording and the automatic mood shift each get tested on their own. Anything that doesn't help gets cut.

Netflix's own product leaders have described judging changes through A/B tests that look at whether members keep watching and stick around. A few weeks of testing like this can show whether people pick better. Whether they stay longer would need a longer experiment.

phase 0 is the cheapest step. it might show this isn't worth building, which would still be a useful answer.

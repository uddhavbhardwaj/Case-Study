Reducing Decision Friction in Streaming Discovery

Why 1 in 5 people give up on picking something to watch, and what I'd try.

Problem
US viewers say they spend about 12 minutes finding something to watch. 1 in 5 say they'd give up if the search fails.
Insight
It's less about too many options and more about not knowing what you want tonight. Netflix adjusts for time and device, but has no way to hear that tonight is different.
Recommendation
When someone browses for a while without picking, add one row of three picks with a reason each, plus chips to say what you want tonight.
Success metric
Share of stuck sessions that end in 10+ minutes of watching, and whether people say it was a good pick.
First step
Confirm the problem with existing data and 20–30 interviews before building anything.

1 · Context

Finding something to watch is getting harder

Gracenote's 2025 State of Play surveyed 3,000 streaming viewers across six countries:

12 minspent searching for something to watch (US), up from 10.5 in 2023
19%would abandon the session if their search failed
45%find the streaming experience overwhelming
49%would consider cancelling a service over it

Self-reported, and across all streaming services, not Netflix alone. Saying you'd cancel isn't cancelling.

What Netflix has tried

  1. 2021Fast Laughs. A vertical feed of comedy clips on mobile.
  2. 2021Play Something. One button, Netflix picks for you.
  3. January 2023Play Something removed for low usage.
  4. 2026Clips. A personalized vertical feed of previews in the mobile app.

Takeaway: people didn't want to hand over the decision, and the newest discovery tool lives on phones.

nearly half would think about cancelling over this. that's the business case.

2 · Problem

Problem statement

Viewers who open Netflix without a clear idea of what they want often browse, don't commit, and leave. The homepage predicts their usual taste well, but gives them no quick way to say tonight is different.

Who I think gets stuck

Tired, end of day

Wants something easy. Their usual picks feel like too much effort tonight.

Watching with someone

Two tastes, one remote. Neither person's usual picks fit both.

Short on time

Has 30 minutes. A 55-minute episode or a two-hour film is out.

These are hypotheses, not validated segments. Phase 0 tests them.

Scope: the TV app. Netflix said in 2018 that about 70% of streams were on TVs. I couldn't find a recent public figure, so TV is my choice of focus, not a proven fact.

3 · Insight

It isn't too many choices

I assumed people freeze because there's too much to choose from. The research doesn't support the simple version:

  • A 2010 meta-analysis of 50 experiments found the average choice-overload effect was close to zero.
  • In recommender studies, bigger sets of good picks didn't raise satisfaction (2010). In a 1.6-million-shopper field test, up to 64% of the drop from showing more products came from people not clicking anything at all (2025).
  • A 2015 review found overload shows up under specific conditions: unclear preferences, a decision that feels like it matters, and needing to choose now.

That last list sounds like a weeknight on the couch:

What the research saysWhat it sounds like at 9pm
People aren't sure what they want
"I don't know what I feel like watching."
They need to pick right now
"I've got an hour before bed."
The decision feels like it matters
"What if I start this and it's bad?"

Netflix already uses time of day, device, watch time and whether you finish things. What it can't see is how you feel tonight. Mood research shows people pick entertainment partly to change their mood: in one classic study, stressed people chose relaxing programs and bored people chose exciting ones.

Hypothesis: some stuck sessions happen when what someone needs tonight doesn't match what the app has guessed. A quick way to say so should help them commit.

this is where I changed my mind. it's also the riskiest assumption here.

4 · Goals and metrics

What success looks like

Goals

  • Help stuck viewers start something they're glad they picked.
  • Work on TV, inside the existing homepage.
  • Keep new releases discoverable.

Non-goals

  • Replacing the homepage or the recommendation system.
  • New content formats, like clips.
  • Changing anything for people who enjoy browsing.
Primary
Share of stuck sessions that end in 10+ minutes of watching.
Quality
Share who say it was a good pick, from a one-question prompt after watching.
Guardrails
Plays of new releases, rewatch share, engagement of viewers who weren't stuck, and how often the row is dismissed.
Long term
30-day retention of people who often get stuck. Needs a long-running holdout group.

7 · Solution

One evening, three moments

How it plays out for one viewer on a Tuesday night. Scroll through, or tap "Lighter tonight" in the screens. Shows and numbers are made up.

9:02pmbrowsing, not picking

nothing changes on screen. the app notices two-plus minutes of hopping between titles, longer than this person usually takes.

9:04pmthe row shows up

one row slides in where their focus already is. everything else stays put.

9:05pmone tap: lighter tonight

same taste, lighter and shorter picks. the comfort pick stays because it already fit.

Design decisions

  • Three slots, each with a job. Safe bet (strongest match), New for you (protects new releases), Comfort (something familiar).
  • Completion is one input, not a quality score. Netflix already uses it as a signal, but finishing something doesn't prove you liked it.
  • Chips ask what you want, not how you feel. "I'm tired" doesn't say whether someone wants comedy or something calm.
  • Reasons get tested. Research shows a reason can make a pick more appealing without helping people choose better. Compare no reason, a tonight-based reason and a history-based one.
  • Nothing else moves. On a remote, where you are on screen is everything.

8 · Risks

Risks and mitigations

RiskMitigation
Why only stuck viewers?If the row helps, maybe everyone should get it.
Test both. I don't know the answer.
Nobody taps the chips.Play Something showed people avoid explicit asks.
Picks adjust on their own first. Chips are a backup, cut if unused.
Reasons sell instead of help.A good-sounding reason can make a bad pick look great.
Judge reasons by "good pick" answers, not clicks.
Completion favors popular, short titles.Recommenders already lean popular, and sitcoms get finished more than films.
Compare within format. Reserve a slot for new releases.
"Stuck" is hard to detect.An idle remote might mean a phone call.
Count only active browsing, compare to each person's normal, start strict.

9 · Validation plan

Validation plan and roadmap

Phase 0 · 2–3 weeks · no engineering

Confirm the problem

  • Data: how often do sessions run 2+ minutes with no play? Do people who do this a lot cancel more?
  • Interviews (20–30): the last time choosing was hard. How were you feeling, who were you with, how much time did you have, and was that night typical? Then a night when choosing was easy, for comparison.

Go only if stuck sessions are common and the stories point to mismatched needs, not something else like a thin catalog.

Phase 1 · 4–5 weeks

Ship the row only

Three-way A/B test: no row, row for everyone, row for stuck viewers. Measure engagement with the row, 10+ minute plays and "good pick" answers separately, against the guardrails in section 4.

Phase 2

Add extras one at a time

Completion signal, chips, reason wording, automatic mood shift. Each has to earn its place.

Netflix evaluates changes through A/B tests focused on engagement and retention. A few weeks shows whether people pick better. Retention needs a longer holdout.

phase 0 might say don't build this. that's still a win.

10 · Open questions

What I'd want to know

  1. Are stuck sessions mostly unusual nights, or something else, like nothing appealing in the catalog?
  2. Should everyone get the row, or only stuck viewers?
  3. Will people tap chips at all, or should the row only adjust on its own?
  4. How do you detect "stuck" on a TV without false positives?

I built this from the outside, so I'd like to hear where it falls apart.
